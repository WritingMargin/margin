The best moment to write documentation is while you still remember what felt confusing.

We often treat documentation as the final wrapper around finished work. Build the feature, resolve the awkward cases, ship it, and then ask someone to explain it. That order hides a useful signal: if a feature is difficult to explain, part of the design may still be unsettled.

## Start with the question

Imagine someone opening a new workspace. They do not yet care how its editor stores content. Their first question is smaller: “Where do I begin?”

A good answer gives them an action and a result. Choose a format. Start a draft. Write a little. Preview it. Save when it feels worth keeping.

The explanation creates a promise. Each screen has to make the next step recognizable enough for the reader to continue without guessing.

> Documentation is a place to test whether the product's promises match its behavior.

## Different reading deserves different shapes

An article invites someone to spend time with an idea. A guide helps them reach an outcome. A playbook helps a team repeat a process. A reference document lets them look up one precise detail and leave.

Those are different reading situations, even when they share the same Markdown editor.

An essay benefits from a quiet reading column. A guide benefits from visible steps and navigation. Reference material needs room for tables, exact values, and examples. Giving each format its own design lets the content do its job without forcing the author to rebuild the page every time.

## Make reuse deliberate

Shared design is valuable when it saves thought that does not need to be repeated: spacing, typography, accessible colors, familiar controls.

But sharing everything can erase useful differences. A coordinated article and guide can have the same visual identity while using different reading widths, heading styles, and navigation placement.

A template should make that relationship explicit. It should say what it supports and supply a real configuration for each supported format.

## Keep a short feedback loop

Publish one small explanation before attempting a complete library. Watch where readers stop, what they search for, and which questions return.

Reading time and scroll depth can help locate a section worth reviewing. They cannot tell you, by themselves, whether someone succeeded. A quick lookup might be a successful visit; a long one might mean the explanation was difficult to follow.

Pair those signals with a simple test: ask someone to use the page to do the task, then watch what happens.

## Leave room for the next reader

Good documentation does not need to sound comprehensive. It needs to help the person who arrived with a question.

Write the first useful answer. Put it in the right shape. Make it easy to find again. Then keep improving it as the product—and your understanding of the reader—changes.
